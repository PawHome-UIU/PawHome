PART 1 - OVERVIEW                                        (about 1 minute)
[Open on page 1, "Full ERD". Let them look at it for two seconds
 before you start talking.]
===============================================================================

Good morning. I am going to walk you through the database design for
PawHome - our pet adoption and boarding management system.

What you are looking at is the complete schema. / Twenty-seven tables,
fifty-three foreign keys, built on MySQL 8.4 with the InnoDB engine.

It looks like a lot. But the whole diagram is organised around just two
tables. [Point to users, then to pets.]

This one is USERS. Twenty of the twenty-seven tables point back to it.
This one is PETS. Nine tables point back to that.

Everything else is one of five areas of the system. Identity and shelters.
Pets, traits and health. The matching quiz. Adoption. And boarding and care.

So the way to read this diagram is not left to right. It is: find the two
hubs, then follow the lines outward. That is what I will do now.


===============================================================================
PART 2 - THE IMPORTANT TABLES AND THEIR KEY ATTRIBUTES   (about 3 minutes)
[Move to page 2, "Identity, shelters and access".]
===============================================================================

--- SHELTERS ---

We start at the root of the whole design: SHELTERS.

This table has no foreign keys at all. Nothing above it. Every other
table in the system eventually traces back here.

Its key attributes are the primary key ID, and a UNIQUE constraint on
NAME - so two shelters cannot register under the same name. It also
carries CAPACITY and BOARDING_RATE_DAILY, because a shelter is not only
an address in this system, it is a place with a limit and a price.

--- USERS ---

Next, USERS. This is the single most important table in the design.

The important decision here is that we did NOT build three tables for
three kinds of people. There is no separate "adopters" table, no separate
"staff" table. There is one USERS table with a ROLE column - an ENUM of
ADOPTER, STAFF or ADMIN. [Point to the role row.]

Why? Because an adopter and a staff member share almost every attribute -
name, email, password, phone. Splitting them would have meant duplicating
all of that, and duplicating the login logic three times.

The key attributes are:

  ID              - primary key
  EMAIL           - UNIQUE. This is what someone logs in with.
  ROLE            - which of the three kinds of account this is
  APPROVAL_STATUS - PENDING, APPROVED or REJECTED
  EMAIL_VERIFIED  - has this person confirmed their address
  IS_ACTIVE       - the kill switch
  SHELTER_ID      - nullable foreign key to shelters

Notice those three separate flags. [Point to approval_status,
email_verified, is_active.] A person can only sign in when all three are
satisfied. They are kept apart on purpose, because each one refuses for a
different reason: an admin has not approved you yet; you never clicked the
link in your email; or your account was switched off.

Now look at SHELTER_ID. It is in italics on the diagram, which means it is
nullable. That is deliberate. Only STAFF belong to a shelter. Every adopter
in the system has NULL here. And there is a CHECK constraint on the table
that enforces exactly that - a row cannot claim to be an ADOPTER and still
name a shelter.

--- PETS ---

[Move to page 3, "Pets, traits and health".]

PETS is the second hub.

  ID          - primary key
  SHELTER_ID  - which shelter is housing this animal
  STATUS      - AVAILABLE, PENDING, ADOPTED, and so on
  OWNER_ID    - nullable
  CREATED_BY  - which staff member listed it

The interesting one is OWNER_ID, and it is nullable for a real reason.
A pet in this system is one of two things. Either it is a shelter animal
waiting to be adopted - and then it has no owner, so this is NULL. Or it
is somebody's own pet that has come in for boarding - and then OWNER_ID
points at that person.

One table, two situations, separated by whether a column is NULL.

--- THE QUIZ TABLES ---

[Move to page 4, "The matching quiz".]

The matching quiz is five tables. QUIZ_QUESTIONS holds the questions.
QUIZ_OPTIONS holds the possible answers to each question. QUIZ_ATTEMPTS is
one person sitting the quiz once. QUIZ_ANSWERS is what they picked.

And OPTION_TRAITS is the scoring rule - it says which personality traits
each answer counts towards. That is how we turn "I live in a small flat
and I work long hours" into a recommendation for a calm, independent cat.

--- ADOPTION ---

[Move to page 5, "Adoption".]

Two tables here, and the difference between them matters.

ADOPTION_APPLICATIONS is a request. Somebody applied. It may be approved,
it may be declined.

ADOPTIONS is the completed fact. The animal actually went home.

We kept them separate because an application is a conversation and an
adoption is a permanent record. Merging them would mean a table where half
the rows are things that never happened.

--- BOARDING ---

[Move to page 6, "Boarding and care".]

BOARDING_REQUESTS is the widest table in the schema - it carries six
foreign keys at once: the pet, the owner, the shelter, the suite, the quiz
attempt, and the staff member who reviewed it. That is because a boarding
booking genuinely does touch six different things.

Hanging off it are BOARDING_INSTRUCTIONS - the owner's care notes,
BOARDING_UPDATES - the photo updates staff post back to the owner, and
CARE_TASKS and CARE_LOGS - what needs doing each day and what actually
got done.

--- SECURITY AND HEALTH ---

Two small tables worth naming.

USER_TOKENS handles email verification and password reset. Look at the
column: TOKEN_HASH, not "token". We never store the link we emailed - only
a SHA-256 of it. If this database ever leaked, it would not be a stack of
live "click here to take over this account" links.

HEALTH_REMINDERS_SENT has a UNIQUE constraint on vaccination plus reminder
kind. That single constraint is what guarantees the system emails an owner
about a due vaccination exactly once, and not every time the job runs.

PART 3 - THE RELATIONSHIPS                               (about 2 minutes)
[Go back to page 1, the Full ERD. Or use page 0 for the notation.]
===============================================================================

Before I describe the relationships - one word on notation.

We used crow's foot notation, in black and white only. The crow's foot is
always on the child - the table that holds the foreign key. A solid line
means the foreign key is NOT NULL, so the parent is mandatory. A dashed
line means it is nullable, so the parent is optional.

Now, the relationships themselves.

ONE-TO-MANY is the shape of most of this diagram:

  One shelter has many staff, many pets, many suites, many adoptions.
  One user files many applications, saves many favourites, and takes
    many quiz attempts.
  One pet has many photos, many vaccinations, and many applications
    against it.

ONE-TO-ONE appears exactly once, and it is worth pointing out.
[Point to the line between adoption_applications and adoptions.]

ADOPTIONS.APPLICATION_ID carries a UNIQUE constraint. Because it is unique,
the many side collapses to one. One application can settle into at most one
adoption. You cannot adopt the same animal twice off the same paperwork -
and that is not enforced by our code, it is enforced by the database.

MANY-TO-MANY appears three times, and I will come back to those in a
moment, because they are the heart of the normalisation.

Finally, OPTIONAL versus MANDATORY. Compare these two lines.
[Point to a solid line, then a dashed one.]

PETS.SHELTER_ID is solid - every pet must be at a shelter. There is no such
thing as an animal in this system with nowhere to be.

USERS.SHELTER_ID is dashed - most users have no shelter, because most users
are adopters.

The line style is telling you a business rule, not just drawing a link.


===============================================================================
PART 4 - NORMALISATION                                   (about 2 minutes)
[Move to page 7, "Normalisation".]
===============================================================================

--- FIRST NORMAL FORM ---

First normal form says: no repeating groups, no lists inside a column.

The shortcut we deliberately did not take was a TRAITS column on the PETS
table holding the text "calm, good with kids, house trained".

That breaks the very first question anyone will ask this database - show me
every calm dog. With a comma-separated string, that becomes a LIKE over
text. It cannot be indexed. And nothing stops "calm" and "Calm" becoming
two different traits.

So every repeating group became its own table: PET_TRAITS, OPTION_TRAITS,
FAVORITES, PET_PHOTOS, BOARDING_INSTRUCTIONS, QUIZ_ANSWERS.

Every cell in this schema holds exactly one value.

--- RESOLVING MANY-TO-MANY ---

That is also how we resolved the three many-to-many relationships.

A pet has many traits, and a trait describes many pets. No relational table
can hold that directly. So PET_TRAITS sits between them, and its primary
key is the pair of foreign keys together - marked PF on the diagram.

[Point to pet_traits.]

The pair being the key is the important part. It means the same pet and the
same trait physically cannot be recorded twice.

The other two are OPTION_TRAITS - between quiz options and traits - and
FAVORITES, between users and pets. These three are the only composite
primary keys in the entire schema.

--- SECOND NORMAL FORM ---

Second normal form only applies where there is a composite key - so it
applies to exactly those three tables.

And they pass it in the simplest possible way: they have no non-key columns
at all. PET_TRAITS is exactly (pet_id, trait_id). There is nothing in them
that could depend on half a key.

The label and the slug of a trait live once, on the TRAITS table.
So renaming "good with kids" is one UPDATE of one row - not an update of
every pet that has it. That is the whole point of the normal form.

--- THIRD NORMAL FORM ---

Third normal form says no column depends on another non-key column.

A shelter's city belongs to SHELTERS - it is not repeated on every pet
housed there. A vaccine's name belongs to VACCINE_TYPES - it is not retyped
on every dose. A question's text belongs to QUIZ_QUESTIONS - it is not
copied into every answer.

Each fact is written in exactly one place, and every other table reaches
it by key.

--- WHERE WE BROKE THE RULE, ON PURPOSE ---

I want to be honest about five places where we do store derivable data,
because a design that claims to be perfectly normalised while the DDL says
otherwise is not a design anyone can check.

  1. ADOPTION_APPLICATIONS copies the applicant's name, email, phone and
     address from USERS. That is not redundancy - it is a snapshot. The
     application must still say where the applicant lived when they
     applied, even after they move house.

  2. ADOPTIONS.SHELTER_ID is reachable through the pet today. But a pet can
     be returned and re-listed at another shelter. The completed adoption
     has to keep naming the shelter that actually handed the animal over.

  3. QUIZ_ATTEMPTS stores SCORE, MAX_SCORE and PASSED, which could be
     recomputed from the answers. We store them because the scoring weights
     on QUIZ_OPTIONS can be edited later, and a past attempt must not
     silently change its result.

  4. PETS.VIEW_COUNT is a counter. Deriving it would mean keeping one row
     per page view - far more storage for a number nobody audits.

  5. PET_PHOTOS.COVER_FLAG and ADOPTION_APPLICATIONS.PENDING_FLAG are
     generated columns. MySQL has no partial unique index, so the flag is
     NULL except on the row that must be unique. That lets UNIQUE enforce
     "at most one cover photo per pet" and "at most one open application
     per person per pet". They are constraints wearing the costume of a
     column.

Every one of those is a deliberate trade, and each one buys something
specific.


===============================================================================
CLOSING                                                  (about 20 seconds)
===============================================================================

So, to summarise.

Twenty-seven tables. Two hubs - users and pets. Five functional areas.

Three many-to-many relationships, all resolved with junction tables.
One true one-to-one, enforced by a UNIQUE constraint.
Third normal form throughout, with five documented exceptions.

The rules of this system are in the schema, not only in the application
code - so the database refuses bad data even if our code forgets to.

Thank you. I am happy to take questions.


===============================================================================
LIKELY QUESTIONS - quick answers
===============================================================================

Q: Why one USERS table instead of separate adopter and staff tables?
A: They share almost every attribute and all of the login logic. One table
   with a ROLE column, plus a CHECK constraint tying SHELTER_ID to STAFF,
   gives us the separation without the duplication.

Q: Why keep applications and adoptions apart?
A: An application is a request that may be declined. An adoption is a
   completed fact. Merging them gives a table where many rows describe
   something that never happened.

Q: Is the schema fully in 3NF?
A: Yes, apart from five documented cases, which are on page 7. Four are
   historical snapshots or counters, and one is a MySQL workaround for the
   lack of a partial unique index.

Q: What are the generated flag columns for?
A: They enforce "at most one" rules. The flag is NULL on every row except
   the one that must be unique, so a normal UNIQUE constraint does the job
   a partial index would do in PostgreSQL.

Q: How is the reminder email prevented from going out twice?
A: HEALTH_REMINDERS_SENT has UNIQUE(vaccination_id, reminder_kind). The
   second attempt to insert the same reminder fails at the database.
